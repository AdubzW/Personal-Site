## Personalities

### Thornstein

Gruff, stern, but fair, Thornstein is a veteran of many battles in which he has learned that careful calculation are the best ways forward. He can sometimes be seen as cold, unfeeling, but he knows intimately what it's like being soldier.

### Franz

Prideful, arrogant, loyal, Franz is a young captain with an inflated sense of purpose and an ego. Elevated by his status, not by ability, he is constantly trying to prove himself to his men and to himself.

### Tim

A quiet, thoughtful man who rarely speaks, unless fawning over a machine, doesn't really matter where from. He was conscripted as the war dragged on into its second year, and placed aboard the U-boat carrying The DyanmoWeapon. Having helped develop the weapon himself, he seemed like the ideal candidate, unless of course he were to be captured.

## Synopsis

The year is 1915, and Captain David Thornstein is on escort duty in the Atlantic, protecting vital British supply lines from the burgeoning threat of German U-boat attack. He would soon find that submarines weren't the only danger that lurked beneath the waves.\
Almost a century before humanity ever knew the horror of the Kaiju, or the triumph of the Jaegers, men, with their inexhaustible hubris, ventured into the depths below. Despite the great technological strides of the age that facilitated their journeys, they did so only by the strength of their backs, and the sweat of their brows. Every movement made, shell loaded, or course plotted, did so only by the will of human hands.\
Cocooned in their great hides of steel, and propelled by great engines of fire, they would do the only thing that they could, face the hurricane, and win.

### Halifax

---

Thousands of Kilometers away from the blood and gore, in the town of Halifax, Canada, great factories churn out their works with predictable efficience; Bullets and shell casing rolling off assembly lines and into weapons bound for Europe.

Through the thick smog of iron and industry, one factory towers above all others. It bears no company markings, has no visible windows, and is surrounded on all sides by rows on rows of gnashing wire. It's entry points are guarded by soldiers marked by the sigil of star and spear, bearing the latest arms, and guarding the greatest innovations that the empire had ever produced. For the townspeople milling about outside its gates, it is the object of intrigue. Erected within mere days, some say that it houses experimental german weapons, stolen, and repurposed for use by her majesty's armed forces.

For those that posses the credentials to pass through its gates unopposed, it is known only by one name: The Sigil.

Inside the bowels of this mighty workshop, workers feed the fires of great furnaces, superheating metal, and pouring the resulting liquid into great molds that span the length of the complex. A floor above them, mechanists assemble great pistons, they themselves below engineers who work tirelessly to wire together machines the likes of which the world has never seen. From deep within the complex great plumes of black smoke bellow, reaching up through the chimneys of the Sigil and out into the blue skies above. There, thousands of miles above, the wayward smoke greets another technical marvel of the age: the aeroplane.

### Thornstein

Thousands of feet below, Captain David Thornstein surveyed the situation from the deck of the [HMS Patrician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Patrician). He gazed up nervously as the survey plane passed by, signaling another 3 hours without further incident.

"Maybe we really did finally kill that thing" he thought to himself, as he pulled out his pipe. Sprinkling in an extra bit of tobacco into the bowl, he proceeded to strike a match on the handrail, and lit pipe. Inhaling, he could feel the hairs on the back of his neck begin to relax.

A young seamen ran up to him from below deck, seeking to deliver a report.

"No sign of any further movement sir" the boy reported. "We've collected all the German survivors and locked them in the brig under strict supervision".

The soldier visibily relaxed, the days of uninterrupted service clearly marking his weary face. He saluted, and retired below deck.

As the captain of the vessel, it now fell to Thornstein to accept the surrender of the detainees, and to provide an after action report to his superiors on the events of the last few days, and the reason as to why the small merchant fleet he and his crew had been tasked to escort now lay decimated mere miles off of the east coast of Canada.

If he would be the one hearing testimony from a sailor such as himself during the inevitable court martial, he would probably think the man insane. Perhaps the 2 recovered u-boats and 17 confirmed destroyed would cast a more favorable light on his first command.

"Sir, with all due respect, these beasts have killed hundreds of our sailors and transports ships. We owe the Hun nothing".

Satisfied with the gesture of submission, Thornstein leaning back towards the German in front of him, Thornstein takes a step forward, stopping to take a puff of his pipe. The German captain grimaces, and for a brief moment Thornstein sees a fire flash across his eyes. Thornstein leans back and roars with laughter. The guards and the interpreter look at each other nervously, and swallow hard. Turning, Thornstein exclaims giddily:

That was a step too far for Franz. As if he was going to let this murderous Kanadier threaten his sacred honor and duty to Germany.  Despite his weariness, he grabbed the Canadian and slammed him against the bulkhead. Through gritted teeth, and with near perfect english, the german captain spat:"

The guards both raised their weapons after the little speech had concluded, but Thornstein bade them to relax. He easily pushed the man back into line, but didn't bother trying to shake his now fiery gaze.

"You might be surprised to know that your little "Shrecken", didn't end down there. It took us 3 dayd, 3 cruisers, and one destroyer to stop it. Even then, god knows whether its really dead".

Thornstein took another puff of his pipe.

"So here's the way I see things. Either you tell us what that thing is, what sort of monstrous kraut lab bore it, and whether there are more like it one the way, or you can join the rest of your jerry buddies at the bottom of the ocean." Thornstein spat coldly.

Adding a melancholic "Savvy?".

Still… the rude captain might have a point, and the acknowledgment of rank and authority from an enemy combatant did fill him with some spirit, despite his intimidating tone. Even Franz's father wouldn't do that. He had been so excited the day he got into the prestigious Murwick Naval Academy, and all his father couldn't even muster a nod.

"If I do give you this information that you desire, you will let me comrades and me go?" he inquired.

Thornstein gave the captain a good look up and down, sizing up his character, then nodded.

"You have my word" he replied.

Their Hydrophones had picked up a massive riiiiip, moments before a shockwave rocked the boat so hard that a few of the subs began to spring deadly leaks. ### Cannon and plan

---

They called it DynamoWaffe. Dynamo weapon. Massive experimental batteries would store excess energy generated by the subs motion, then focus that energy into a highly concentrated beam. Against shipping, it would have been devestating. Entire crews and their ships, fried almost instantly if any of them were touching metal when the surge hit. Any survivors would find their electronic equipment, radios, generators, and most importantly, engines, fried and inoperable. A sitting duck for torpedo fire.

Thornstein shuddered at the thought. That the Germans could have developed a weapon such as this even with their metal supplies exhausted and their northern ports effectively blockaded? He was glad their were helping. Hopefully the eggheads back at home knew what to make of it.

Franz and the engineer, Muller, had volunteered to escort their still intact version back to Canada, on the condition that they themselves be marked as killed in action in the ship's report, and that they be present with the weapon at all times. For these men, death was worse than betraying their country.

It took a whole two weeks to get a the proper discharge papers for himself and his immediate crew. As expected, admiralty was not happy to hear about their lost cruisers, but warmed up when they heard about the captured German subs. He hadn't mentioned the weapon yet. Thornstein had spent the past two weeks getting to know his two German counterparts, and found that they had a lot in common. He didn't take his duty lightly, and if pushed, he'd always turn first to the crown. But of what the crown did not know, the crown could not ask.

Even better knews had come in days before their arrival in Halifax. Project Sigil was ready for Launch. Mere days after the crew reported its first contact with the beast, word had been sent up the chain of command. Thornstein still had some strings he could pull with the admiralty, and almost immediately, a factory had been built, resources requisitioned, and engineers assembled to build a project that the public would hopefully never learn about.

"You won't believe it when you see it" Thornstein had told Franz and Janis as they boarded they stepped off the Halifax dock.

The german captain scoffed. Nothing could possibly outmatch the German army engineers.

"You know it too Englander, in mere month Northern France will be subsumed into the reich. You may even have to learn German!" he exclaimed gleefully.

Suffice to say, the german would change his tune.

Their jaws fell halfway to the floor when they finally saw the launchpad. Thornstein couldn't help but chuckle as he leaned back in his chair and drew in a puff of his pipe.

"That boys, is the GPSD-1, first fully submersible mechanically assisted diving automaton, or so the eggheads back at base inform me." he explained.

What he failed to mention was that the machine had never been fully tested. Teams from across the commonwealth had been hastily assembled shortly after the first encounter. Fearing further such attacks, and suspecting newfangled german war tactics, high command had immediately ordered that all prototype tanks, fighters, and the newly captured submarines from the first recorded breach be put at the disposal of the team in Halifax.

Their codename was Sigil. Their initial purpose before the first breach was to counter german subs, so they already had a head start on production and assembly. Their creations? Jeagers, they called them; hunters. The reason? German U-boats hunted in what they called "packs", so it was the hunter's job to keep the wolves away from the sheep. When the team's mandate shifted, well, the name stuck.

They were fitted with lightweight backup breathing gear and loaded into the main cabin by ramp. Once inside, the top porthole was sealed behind them from the outside. Thornstein shuffled nervously. One of the sailors near him told him not to worry, that this was standard procedure, and that the compartment would be resealed upon surfacing. The test would last 45 minutes.

Outside he could see massive reinforced oxygen, power, and communications being seated into various ports around the hull. Two of each, for redundancy, in case one or the other failed. In total, over 1000 meters of cabled were to be lowered down with the Jaeger when it embarked on its first mission. Above them, he could see the minesweeper Artimedes \[citation needed] spring to life as its powerful engines prepared to lower the beast down from its retrofitted holding area specially made for the jaeger.

Crewman were milling about around them, slipping through portholes and squeezing into tight compartments to operate the complex servos and hydraulics that would spur the Jaeger to motion. On the command deck their was the chief engineer, the captain and crew, and their entourage of visitors and observers.

With a clank, the cranes began to lower the machine into the water, and quickly, light faded to darkness. With a thud, the machine hit the shallow bay of Halifax. A crewman yelled out "clear", as the machines forward lights flashed to life. Thornstein could recognize the controls being used. It appeared as though they hadn't even had the time to re-engineer anything new. The entire command deck was unchanged from when he had helmed it.

The elderly captain grumbled a system's check command. First the leg compartment flexed the machine up and down, first slowly, then quicker. "Legs nominal" the crew chief announced.

Then the arms. He marvelled as they too began to flex back and forth. "Arms nominal" came over the jeager's loudspeakers. Finally, communications, navigation, and hydrograph would announce their affirmative as well. If Thornstein had closed his eyes, he would have been able to imagine himself on his own bridge. He could almost smell the salt water.

The captain gave the order to advance, and the machine lurched to life. Within a few short minutes they had descended another 20 feet into the bay. A further lumbering 20, and they were in firing position.

"Firing positions" the captain muttered, and an orderly repeated the command into his radio. Servos ground as the machine lowered its bulky frame further. Suddenly, alarm bells began to sound all around. One of the arm compartments had sprung a leak. The servos ground to a stop, and water began spraying out of the gap. The old man barely seemed to react. Bored, he waved to his lieutenant who nervously ordered the emergency crews to fill the gap with foam sealant.

Franz looked shocked, and his eyes shot daggers at the old man.

"That foam isn't going to hold back the pressure" he cried, turning to Thornstein; "that man is going to kill us all!". The sound reverberated through the tight compartment. The crewman nearby glanced back nervously.

"Don't any of you know what you're doing he cried?".

"Ignore him" The captain ordered, more firmly now, "Fill the gap and continue, that's an order".

Franz plead with Thornstein, but whispered more quietly now. "The man is blind" he hissed. "That whole compartment is compromised, any second now it could blow and implode the whole section".

Thornstein glared back, but he knew the man was right. Although he had never served aboard a sub like Franz, he could see that the old man was clearly unqualified for the job.

The Jeager shuddered as it assumed crouching position. Crewman hoisted themselves up into the firing compartment and started priming the gauss cannon. A thin beam of light focused through a small lense was shot out and projected onto an underwater mine target. The firing officer began punching data into targeting computer corresponding to the distance and elevation of the target relative to the Jaeger. Around them, guages began the fill as the weapon charged. Engineering compartments called in positive systems checks, as the supercapacitors whirred louder and louder.
